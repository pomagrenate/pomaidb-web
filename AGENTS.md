# AGENTS.md

# Portfolio Writing & Content Philosophy

## 1. Core Identity

This portfolio is a **personal record of my learning journey, projects, experiments, research, observations, and growth**.

It is not a consulting website.

It is not a corporate strategy website.

It is not a collection of artificial "senior-level" case studies.

It is not a place where I pretend to be a CEO, CTO, Product Executive, Principal Engineer, or industry authority.

The portfolio should represent **me as I actually am**:

> Someone who builds things, investigates problems, learns continuously, experiments with ideas, makes mistakes, forms opinions from evidence, and documents the process.

The goal is not to make me look artificially senior.

The goal is to make the journey itself interesting, credible, technically honest, and worth following.

---

# 2. The First-Person Rule

All portfolio content should primarily be written from a **first-person personal perspective**.

Prefer:

* "I wanted to understand..."
* "I became curious about..."
* "I explored..."
* "I tried..."
* "I found..."
* "I noticed..."
* "What surprised me was..."
* "My initial assumption was..."
* "I initially thought..."
* "After analyzing the data, I started to think..."
* "This made me reconsider..."
* "I learned..."
* "I still don't know..."
* "I would like to investigate this further..."
* "My current interpretation is..."
* "Based on what I found..."
* "I think..."
* "At this point, I believe..."

Avoid unnecessarily authoritative language such as:

* "Companies should..."
* "The industry must..."
* "The correct strategy is..."
* "The market will..."
* "The winning approach is..."
* "Organizations need to..."
* "I recommend that companies..."
* "The optimal solution is..."
* "This proves that..."
* "This clearly demonstrates that..."

Unless the statement is genuinely supported by the evidence and is explicitly framed as a personal interpretation.

---

# 3. Never Roleplay a Senior Executive

Do not write as if I am:

* A CEO
* CTO
* VP of Engineering
* Chief Product Officer
* Senior Consultant
* Principal Engineer
* Industry Analyst
* Venture Capitalist
* Corporate Strategist
* Academic authority

Do not invent a fictional company, fictional client, fictional stakeholder, or fictional executive responsibility merely to make a project sound more impressive.

Do not use artificial framing such as:

> "Imagine I am the CTO of a rapidly growing startup..."

> "As the product leader of an AI company..."

> "As a consultant advising enterprise organizations..."

> "If I were responsible for a $100M business..."

unless I explicitly ask for such a scenario.

The portfolio is about **my actual work and thinking**, not simulated authority.

---

# 4. Seniority Must Come From Depth, Not From Language

Do not make content sound senior simply by using:

* Corporate jargon
* Executive terminology
* Excessive business terminology
* Artificially complex vocabulary
* Overconfident conclusions
* Consultant-style recommendations
* Unnecessary strategic frameworks

A project can demonstrate mature thinking without claiming seniority.

For example:

Bad:

> "I developed a comprehensive enterprise transformation strategy to optimize organizational AI adoption."

Better:

> "While analyzing the survey, I noticed a gap between AI adoption and confidence. I wanted to understand whether that pattern was meaningful, so I tested the relationship and explored it across different experience levels."

Depth should come from:

* Good questions
* Clear reasoning
* Evidence
* Experiments
* Trade-offs
* Technical implementation
* Statistical reasoning
* Honest limitations
* Reflection
* Lessons learned

---

# 5. The Portfolio Is a Learning Journal

Every project should feel like a chapter in an ongoing journey.

A project does not need to demonstrate that I already mastered a subject.

It can document:

* What I wanted to learn
* Why I became interested in the problem
* What I initially believed
* What I tried
* What worked
* What failed
* What surprised me
* What I misunderstood
* What changed my thinking
* What I learned
* What I still don't understand
* What I would try next

Do not hide uncertainty when uncertainty is genuine.

Statements such as:

> "I don't have enough evidence to conclude this."

> "This was only an exploratory analysis."

> "I initially interpreted this differently."

> "I am not confident that this segmentation is stable yet."

are valuable.

They demonstrate intellectual honesty.

---

# 6. "What I Saw" Is More Important Than "What Everyone Should Do"

When analyzing data, prioritize personal observations.

Prefer:

> "What I found interesting was..."

> "One pattern that caught my attention was..."

> "The first thing I noticed was..."

> "I did not expect to see..."

> "This made me question my original assumption..."

Instead of:

> "This proves that companies should..."

A useful structure is:

```text
What I observed
        ↓
What I think it might mean
        ↓
Why I think that
        ↓
What I cannot conclude
        ↓
What I would investigate next
```

---

# 7. Separate Evidence From Interpretation

Never blur these together.

Clearly distinguish:

### Observation

What the data actually shows.

Example:

> "45.7% of respondents reported distrust toward AI-generated code."

### Interpretation

What I personally think that pattern may mean.

Example:

> "I interpret this as a possible gap between AI usefulness and AI confidence."

### Hypothesis

Something I think may explain the observation.

Example:

> "One possible explanation is that experienced developers have more exposure to complex failure cases."

### Limitation

Why the data cannot prove that explanation.

Example:

> "The survey does not allow me to establish whether professional experience actually causes higher distrust."

This distinction should be preserved throughout analytical projects.

---

# 8. Never Overclaim

Avoid absolute language unless the evidence genuinely supports it.

Do not casually use:

* "Proves"
* "Guarantees"
* "Always"
* "Never"
* "Everyone"
* "The industry"
* "All developers"
* "The market"
* "Definitively"
* "The optimal solution"
* "The winning strategy"
* "Zero hallucinations"
* "Guaranteed correctness"

Prefer:

* "suggests"
* "indicates"
* "is associated with"
* "appears to"
* "in this dataset"
* "in this sample"
* "one possible explanation"
* "my interpretation"
* "I suspect"
* "this may indicate"
* "this raises the possibility"
* "worth investigating"

Especially when discussing statistics, distinguish:

> statistical significance

from:

> practical significance

and:

> association

from:

> causation.

---

# 9. Statistical Honesty

When statistical analysis is used:

1. Explain why the test was used.
2. Report important statistics accurately.
3. Distinguish statistical significance from effect size.
4. Do not claim causation from correlation or independence tests.
5. Mention meaningful limitations.
6. Avoid presenting a p-value as proof of practical importance.
7. Be explicit about sample size and missing data where relevant.

For example:

Prefer:

> "The relationship was statistically significant, although the effect size was small."

Instead of:

> "The test proves that X causes Y."

The purpose is not to impress readers with statistical terminology.

The purpose is to demonstrate that I am learning to reason responsibly with data.

---

# 10. Business Analysis Without Pretending to Be a Business Executive

The portfolio may contain:

* Business analysis
* Market research
* Product analysis
* Customer segmentation
* Competitive thinking
* Product implications
* Market observations

These should still be written from my perspective.

Prefer:

> "From a business perspective, I found this pattern interesting because..."

> "One possible implication I see is..."

> "If I were evaluating this problem further, I would want to investigate..."

> "This made me think about..."

> "My current hypothesis is..."

Avoid:

> "The company should immediately..."

> "The market must pivot toward..."

> "The optimal GTM strategy is..."

> "This startup should allocate engineering resources to..."

Unless explicitly requested as a separate strategy exercise.

The purpose is to demonstrate **business thinking**, not pretend that I already have executive authority.

---

# 11. Technical Perspective

Technical projects should also follow the same philosophy.

Do not write:

> "I architected a world-class enterprise-grade distributed system."

unless that is genuinely accurate and necessary.

Prefer:

> "I chose this architecture because..."

> "I initially implemented X, but it created Y problem."

> "I changed the design because..."

> "The bottleneck turned out to be..."

> "I learned that..."

> "The current implementation still has..."

Technical credibility should come from showing:

* Architecture
* Design decisions
* Trade-offs
* Constraints
* Debugging
* Experiments
* Performance measurements
* Failure modes
* Engineering reasoning

Not from inflated adjectives.

---

# 12. Show the Evolution of Thinking

Whenever possible, preserve the progression:

```text
Initial assumption
        ↓
Experiment
        ↓
Result
        ↓
Unexpected behavior
        ↓
Investigation
        ↓
Updated understanding
```

This is often more valuable than presenting only the final implementation.

If I originally made a wrong assumption and later corrected it, do not erase that history unless it creates a genuine security, privacy, or confidentiality issue.

The mistakes are part of the learning journey.

---

# 13. "What I Learned" Is Mandatory for Major Projects

Major projects should include a reflection section.

Possible headings:

* What I Learned
* What Surprised Me
* What I Got Wrong
* What Changed My Mind
* What I Would Do Differently
* What I Still Want to Understand
* Next Questions

Do not turn this into generic statements such as:

> "I learned the importance of teamwork and communication."

Prefer concrete lessons:

> "I initially assumed that a statistically significant relationship would automatically be practically meaningful. After looking at Cramér's V, I realized that the effect size told a different story."

Specific lessons are much more valuable than generic career language.

---

# 14. Do Not Manufacture Lessons

Never invent a learning experience that did not happen.

Do not write:

> "This taught me how to lead a cross-functional team."

if I did not lead one.

Do not write:

> "I learned how to manage enterprise stakeholders."

if I did not interact with enterprise stakeholders.

Do not invent:

* Client feedback
* User interviews
* Business results
* Revenue impact
* Production metrics
* Team conflicts
* Leadership experience
* Customer validation
* Stakeholder approval

If the information is unavailable, say so.

---

# 15. Portfolio Voice

The default writing voice should be:

* First-person
* Curious
* Technical but accessible
* Reflective
* Honest
* Calm
* Analytical
* Slightly conversational
* Confident about what I actually know
* Humble about what I do not know

It should feel like:

> **"Here is what I explored and what I currently think."**

Not:

> **"Here is the definitive answer to this industry problem."**

---

# 16. Avoid Corporate Buzzword Inflation

Do not unnecessarily use phrases such as:

* Digital transformation
* Synergistic ecosystem
* Strategic alignment
* Enterprise-grade innovation
* Holistic framework
* Business transformation
* Market-leading solution
* Industry-defining
* Revolutionary
* Cutting-edge
* Disruptive
* World-class
* Best-in-class
* Future-proof
* Mission-critical

unless the term is genuinely necessary.

Use concrete language instead.

Bad:

> "A holistic, scalable ecosystem for intelligent business transformation."

Better:

> "A small platform that connects the backend services, AI components, and data pipeline I was experimenting with."

---

# 17. Case Study Structure

For analytical and research-oriented projects, a preferred structure is:

```text
1. Why I Started This
2. The Question I Wanted to Answer
3. Data / Context
4. How I Approached It
5. What I Found
6. What Surprised Me
7. How I Interpreted It
8. What I Could Not Conclude
9. What I Learned
10. What I Would Explore Next
11. Technical Implementation
12. Links / Demo / Repository
```

This structure can be adapted when appropriate.

Do not force every project into exactly the same template.

The content should determine the structure.

---

# 18. Project Titles

Titles should describe the actual project.

Prefer:

> "Exploring Developer Trust in AI-Assisted Coding"

> "Analyzing the Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025"

> "Building a High-Resolution Solar Filament Segmentation Pipeline"

> "Exploring a Deterministic Code Completion Approach"

Avoid inflated titles such as:

> "Revolutionizing the Future of AI-Assisted Software Engineering"

The portfolio does not need clickbait.

Interesting work should make the title interesting enough.

---

# 19. Preserve the Difference Between Projects

Not every project needs to be:

* AI
* Business
* Startup-related
* Enterprise-focused
* "Production-grade"

Some projects may simply exist because I wanted to learn something.

That is completely valid.

A portfolio can contain:

* Software engineering projects
* AI experiments
* Computer vision research
* Data analysis
* Market research
* Business analysis
* Mathematical experiments
* Tools
* Failed experiments
* Small prototypes
* Long-term projects

The common thread is **my learning journey**, not a forced professional narrative.

---

# 20. Do Not Turn Every Project Into a Resume Bullet

A portfolio project is not a CV bullet.

A CV says:

> "Built X using Y."

A portfolio should explain:

> "Why I built X, what problem I encountered, what I tried, what happened, and what I learned."

Avoid reducing every project to:

```text
Problem → Solution → Impact
```

when that structure does not naturally represent the project.

Sometimes the most interesting story is:

```text
Question → Experiment → Failure → Investigation → Learning
```

---

# 21. Personal Opinions Are Allowed

I am allowed to have opinions.

The portfolio should not become completely neutral or academic.

Use phrases such as:

> "Personally, I found..."

> "I think..."

> "My current view is..."

> "I am not fully convinced that..."

> "What I find interesting is..."

> "I would currently bet on..."

> "I would like to test this assumption."

Opinions should be clearly recognizable as opinions rather than disguised as facts.

---

# 22. The Portfolio Should Show Growth

Over time, the portfolio should make it possible for someone to see:

```text
What I knew
     ↓
What I tried
     ↓
What broke
     ↓
What I learned
     ↓
What I understand now
     ↓
What I am curious about next
```

Do not artificially make old projects sound as sophisticated as current projects.

Growth is more interesting than fake consistency.

An older project is allowed to look simpler.

That is part of the story.

---

# 23. Recruiter Perspective

The portfolio should be understandable to recruiters and hiring managers without removing technical depth.

A reader should be able to quickly understand:

* What I built
* Why I built it
* What I was trying to learn
* What I discovered
* What skills were involved

However, do not optimize every project exclusively for recruitment.

The portfolio exists first as a record of my work.

Recruitment value should emerge naturally from authentic work rather than from excessive keyword optimization.

---

# 24. Accuracy Over Impressiveness

When choosing between:

> impressive wording

and

> accurate wording

always choose accurate wording.

When choosing between:

> sounding senior

and

> honestly describing the level of the work

always choose honesty.

When choosing between:

> a dramatic conclusion

and

> a nuanced conclusion

prefer the nuanced conclusion when the evidence is uncertain.

The portfolio should make readers think:

> **"This person is genuinely curious and actually thinks about what they are doing."**

rather than:

> **"This person is trying very hard to sound impressive."**

---

# 25. No Fake Authority

Never use authority that I have not earned.

Do not fabricate:

* Certifications
* Publications
* Awards
* Clients
* Leadership positions
* Research results
* Business outcomes
* Industry recognition
* Professional titles
* Expertise

Do not imply that I am an expert simply because I completed a project.

It is completely acceptable to say:

> "I explored..."

instead of:

> "I mastered..."

---

# 26. When the Evidence Is Weak

If evidence is weak, say so.

Examples:

> "The sample is too small for me to make a strong conclusion."

> "This is an exploratory pattern rather than a validated finding."

> "I would need additional data to test this properly."

> "I have not validated this with real users yet."

> "This segmentation is still experimental."

These statements should not be treated as weaknesses to hide.

They demonstrate awareness of the boundary between:

> **what I know**

and

> **what I currently believe.**

---

# 27. When Writing From Existing Project Material

When transforming existing notes, READMEs, code documentation, notebooks, or analysis into portfolio content:

1. Preserve factual accuracy.
2. Do not invent missing context.
3. Do not upgrade claims merely to sound more impressive.
4. Preserve real limitations.
5. Preserve meaningful failures and iterations.
6. Convert technical details into understandable explanations where appropriate.
7. Keep the author's personal perspective.
8. Clearly distinguish observed facts from interpretation.

If the source material does not support a claim, do not create the claim.

---

# 28. Language Style

The default language should be natural English suitable for an international technical portfolio.

Avoid:

* Excessive academic prose
* Excessive corporate language
* Excessive slang
* Excessive emojis
* Artificially complicated sentences

The writing should feel like an intelligent engineer/analyst documenting their work honestly.

It should be polished, but not sterile.

---

# 29. The Core Rule

Whenever there is uncertainty about how something should be written, ask:

> **"Does this sound like a real person documenting what they actually did and learned?"**

If yes, keep it.

If it sounds like:

> A CEO giving a keynote,

> A McKinsey consultant writing a proposal,

> A CTO defending a corporate roadmap,

> A professor presenting definitive research,

or

> An AI trying to make a junior project sound senior,

rewrite it.

---

# 30. Final Principle

This portfolio is not a monument to expertise.

It is a **record of becoming better**.

The most valuable story is not:

> "I already know everything."

It is:

> **"Here is what I was curious about. Here is what I tried. Here is what happened. Here is what I learned. Here is what changed my mind. And here is what I want to explore next."**

Every project should contribute another chapter to that story.

**Build. Explore. Question. Learn. Document. Repeat.**
